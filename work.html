Selected work

Four engagements, written up as short case studies. Each one carries a label that says whether I led the work, co-authored it or contributed to a team effort. Client and partner details are limited to what is already public.

ClimaFii: gender inside the commercial work

Gender technical assistance, climate enterprises, go-to-market, financing

Workstream lead, Gender Technical Assistance
  • 10climate enterprises in the gender TA workstream
  • 20,000women micro-entrepreneurs targeted by December 2026
Context

ClimaFii is a partnership between Accion and BFA Global, funded by Shell Foundation and the Gates Foundation. It supports two cohorts of clean energy enterprises in Sub-Saharan Africa and India, with a target of reaching 20,000 women micro-entrepreneurs by December 2026.

The problem

Gender work inside enterprise programmes often runs as a separate track: a policy, a training, a report. It sits next to product, pricing and sales without changing any of them. The question for this workstream was how to put gender into the decisions an enterprise already has to make.

My role

I lead the Gender TA workstream across the enterprises, working alongside my BFA Global counterparts and the commercial technical assistance teams.

Approach

I built a gender intentionality assessment that can be repeated across enterprises and adapted to each one. It looks at product design, customer acquisition, affordability and financing, go-to-market and data. Each partner receives a formal report with a score, a radar chart and areas for improvement, written to recognise what already sets that enterprise apart. Gender support is then layered into the enterprise’s existing ClimaFii workstreams in place of a standalone programme.

Contribution

The assessment tools, the report template, the way gender is written into each enterprise’s scope of work, and the field tools used for visits. I run the assessments and follow them with enterprise-specific technical assistance.

What the work keeps pointing to

Uptake and repayment rarely tell the whole story. A woman can take a loan she did not ask for, or make digital payments in her name, while decisions at home stay where they were. Success measures have to include whether women can say no to a product they do not want.

Why it matters for the sector

When gender sits inside an enterprise’s commercial plan, it competes for the same attention as pricing and distribution, which is where enterprise decisions get made.

Green Worms: does the rainfall trigger match the shock?

Parametric insurance, climate risk, product analysis, field research

Field research and product analysis, with the ClimaFii Resilience Innovation Lab team
  • 2days of field research in Thamarassery and Wayanad
Context

Green Worms is a waste management enterprise in Kerala. Its Haritha Karma Sena collectives are women who collect waste house to house, scanning QR codes to update records as they go. Under ClimaFii, with Accion, Shell Foundation and Upaya Social Ventures, the team is exploring parametric rain insurance for these workers.

The problem

Heavy rain, floods and landslides interrupt the work of people with very little room to absorb a shock. A parametric cover pays out when a rainfall threshold is crossed. Whether that threshold matches what workers actually lose is a question of product design.

My role

I contributed to the rainfall and payout analysis and joined the field research and awareness-building work. The parametric product work is a team effort within the Resilience Innovation Lab.

Approach

Analysis: rainfall patterns, policy periods and payout triggers, checking whether the triggers were realistically reachable within the policy period. Field: focus group discussions and one-to-one conversations about the product with women who had often never heard of insurance. We also ran a simulation game with glasses, rainfall levels, dice and mock payouts, so workers could test how a trigger works.

What the field showed

The same rainfall event hits workers differently. In Wayanad, when families are evacuated, there are no households to collect from, so the work itself disappears for a while.

The cost of a shock also hides in recovery. Workers described making up for lost days with longer hours afterwards, and some spend flood days in relief camps without pay. Counting only the days of lost income misses that.

By the end of the game, workers were asking questions, testing scenarios and saying they would pay for the cover.

Why it matters for the sector

The path from climate event to lost income deserves as much study as the climate event. Building understanding is part of building the product, because a cover people cannot test is a cover they will not buy.

IFC Philippines: making women visible in the data and in the decisions

Sex-disaggregated data, financial institutions, institutional strategy

Co-host of the Manila roundtable, contributor to the assignment
  • 57%of Filipino women hold an account, against 43% of men
Context

In the Philippines, women are ahead of men on account ownership. The gaps sit in usage, credit and business growth, and credit by gender is still poorly measured. Accion is working with IFC on how financial institutions can strengthen their gender data foundations.

The problem

Many institutions already collect data on women customers. Collecting it, analysing it and using it to change business decisions are three different things, and the data often stops at the first.

My role

I co-hosted a roundtable in Manila with IFC, together with my Accion colleagues Devika Misra and Varun Chitale, on sex-disaggregated data and women’s financial inclusion. I also contributed to the wider assignment.

What the room kept returning to

Gender data can sit in reporting or sustainability teams without reaching the people who decide on products, pricing, risk and growth. Risk and finance teams want to see repayment, retention and loan size by sex before backing a new product.

A credible business case avoids generalisations about women as borrowers or customers. It needs to show how performance varies across segments, products and contexts.

A dashboard can say how many women an institution serves. Finding where women drop out of the customer journey, which requirement removes them and why they leave takes a different level of analysis. Using bureau and alternative data without building in bias is part of that.

Why it matters for the sector

The aim is for visibility to change decisions. That means connecting better data to better questions, and better questions to better financial services for women.

Bridging the usage gap: from holding a phone to making a payment

Research, digital financial inclusion, inclusive design, India

Co-author, with Aathira Krishnan
  • 4,340low-income women surveyed across six Indian states
  • 95%have access to a phone
  • 25%made a digital payment in the last six months
  • 72%have never transacted digitally
Context

Accion Advisory carried out this research with the support of HSBC India. Alongside the survey, the team ran focus group discussions and key informant interviews with women and with the people they turn to for support.

The problem

Phone access is nearly universal in the sample. Digital payment use is not. What sits between the two?

My role

I co-authored the report with Aathira Krishnan. Accion’s wider team contributed to the survey and fieldwork.

Approach

We grouped women into four digital personas along a spectrum and looked at what moves a woman from one to the next.

  1. NascentNo personal phone. In many households, learning digital tools is not seen as possible.
  2. ExperimenterUses a shared phone, often her husband’s. Knows UPI, has little or no experience of transacting herself.
  3. EmergerOwns a smartphone and makes small, regular payments, often from memorised steps.
  4. EnabledTransacts independently and often becomes the unofficial tech support for other women.
What we found

The Experimenters are the group to watch. Only about 1% have ever made a digital payment, and one moment holds many of them back: entering the OTP or PIN. About a third of women overall are comfortable doing this without help. Among Experimenters it is one in four.

What helps is someone they already trust: a self-help group leader, a bank staff member or Sakhi, an Anganwadi worker or a neighbour.

“A system voice feels like technology. A human-like voice feels like help.”Finding shared in Accion’s research with HSBC India

Why it matters for the sector

Access alone does not produce use. Women need the chance to practise, make a first transaction safely and know that someone will be there if something goes wrong. Products, programmes and policy for low-income women can be designed around that threshold.
